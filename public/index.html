What is Islam?

A short, honest overview for anyone who is curious. Take it slowly, and stop whenever you like.

Being checked with our scholars

We follow the structure the Prophet ﷺ himself gave when the angel Jibril asked him about the religion: Islam (what we do), iman (what we believe) and ihsan (how we do it, with excellence). It is in Sahih Muslim, hadith 8.

The one truth

There is one God, who created everything, and He alone deserves worship.

Every prophet called to this same message: “We sent no messenger before you except that We revealed to him: there is no god but Me, so worship Me” (Quran 21:25). Islam is the way of submitting to Him alone: “Indeed, the religion in the sight of Allah is Islam” (Quran 3:19).

We state what Muslims believe plainly and with confidence. For the evidence and the questions people ask, see Go deeper and Questions and doubts.

The short answers

What is my purpose?

To know Allah and worship Him (Quran 51:56).

What did Allah create me for?

“My prayer, my sacrifice, my living and my dying are for Allah, Lord of the worlds” (6:162).

What is worship?

Ibn Taymiyyah defined it as everything Allah loves and is pleased with, in words and deeds, inward and outward. So it is a whole life lived for Him, not only ritual.

What is La ilaha illallah?

There is no god but Allah. Only He deserves worship. Everything else in Islam grows from this.

Who is the Prophet ﷺ?

Muhammad, the final messenger and seal of the prophets (33:40).

The heart of it

Islam means willing submission to Allah, the One God who created everything. Muslims believe every human being is born with a natural inclination to recognise their Creator. This is called the fitra.

وَمَا خَلَقْتُ الْجِنَّ وَالْإِنسَ إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُونِ

“I did not create jinn and mankind except to worship Me.”

Quran 51:56

The Prophet ﷺ said every child is born upon the fitra (Sahih al-Bukhari 1385, Sahih Muslim 2658). So for many people, coming to Islam feels less like arriving somewhere new and more like coming home. See also Quran 30:30.

Islam: what we do

The five pillars (Sahih al-Bukhari 8, Sahih Muslim 16).

Shahadah

Declaring there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad ﷺ is His messenger.

Salah

The five daily prayers.

Zakat

Giving a set portion of savings to those in need.

Sawm

Fasting in the month of Ramadan.

Hajj

Pilgrimage to Makkah, once, for those able.

ShahadaPrayerZakatFastingHajj

Iman: what we believe

Six articles of faith (Sahih Muslim 8): belief in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and the divine decree.

Allah

One, with no partner. Tawheed is the foundation of everything.

Angels

Honoured creation who obey Allah.

Books

Revelation sent to the prophets, ending with the Quran.

Messengers

From Adam to Muhammad ﷺ, all calling to the same message.

The Last Day

We will return to Allah and be answered for what we did.

The decree

Nothing happens outside Allah’s knowledge and wisdom.

Ihsan: doing it well

The Prophet ﷺ described ihsan as worshipping Allah as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, knowing that He sees you (Sahih Muslim 8). It is what turns the actions into a relationship.

The Prophet and the Quran

Muhammad ﷺ is the final messenger (Quran 33:40). The Quran is the word of Allah, revealed in stages (Sahih al-Bukhari 4993) and preserved by Him:

“Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian.”

Quran 15:9

You do not have to take it all at once

Why we go step by step

When the Prophet ﷺ sent Mu’adh to Yemen, he told him to call people first to the shahadah, then to prayer, then to zakat, step by step (Sahih al-Bukhari 1496, Sahih Muslim 19). He also said: “Make things easy and do not make them hard” (Sahih al-Bukhari 69, Sahih Muslim 1734).

What is our responsibility?

Surah al-Asr is the shortest answer. Loss is the default. The way out is faith, good deeds, and helping one another stay on the truth and be patient.

وَالْعَصْرِ ﴿١﴾ إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ لَفِي خُسْرٍ ﴿٢﴾ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْحَقِّ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ ﴿٣﴾

“By time, indeed mankind is in loss, except those who believe and do righteous deeds and advise each other to truth and advise each other to patience.”

Quran 103:1-3

That is why this project exists. Guidance is Allah’s alone: “You do not guide whom you like, but Allah guides whom He wills” (28:56). Support is our part.

Why do some of us say “revert”?
Being checked with our scholars

Many Muslims say “revert” because Islam teaches that every person is born on the fitrah, a natural state of turning to Allah. Allah says: “So direct your face toward the religion, inclining to truth: the fitrah of Allah upon which He created people” (Quran 30:30). The Prophet ﷺ said that every child is born on the fitrah (reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). On that view, someone who comes to Islam is returning to it.

You will also hear “convert” and “new Muslim”. Everyone understands all of them. Use whichever word you are comfortable with. You are welcome here either way.

Taking your shahada

Becoming Muslim is simple and free, and you can do it anywhere. You do not need to know everything first. If you have questions, ask them before you decide. Nobody will rush you.

أَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ، وَأَشْهَدُ أَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا رَسُولُ اللَّهِ

Ash-hadu an la ilaha illallah, wa ash-hadu anna Muhammadan rasulullah

I bear witness that there is no god but Allah, and I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah.

The first part says that Allah alone is God, and that worship belongs to Him alone. The second says that Muhammad ﷺ is the messenger He sent, and that we follow his guidance. Say it because you mean it. It is between you and Allah.

How people do it

At a masjid, with someone beside you

Many people choose this, so there is a witness, a welcome, and someone to answer questions. A masjid can often give you a certificate too, which can help later with things like marriage or Hajj paperwork. Find a circle near you.

With us

Email us and we will try to connect you with someone who can sit with you. We are based in Manchester. hubrevert@gmail.com

On your own

You can say it by yourself, anywhere, in your own time. If you do, please tell someone afterwards, so you are not left alone with it.

Common questions

Do I need a witness, or a special person?

You do not need a ceremony or a special person. Many people choose to say it with someone beside them, because it helps to have a witness and a welcome.

Do I have to go to a masjid?

No, but it helps. A masjid gives you people, answers, and somewhere to start learning to pray.

Do I need to know Arabic?

No. Many people repeat the Arabic after someone and then say the meaning in their own language. If you have a particular concern, ask a scholar.

Do I need to know how to pray first?

No. Prayer and everything else are learned afterwards, step by step.

Does it cost anything?

No. Nobody should ever charge you to become Muslim.

What about a bath, called ghusl?

Many scholars recommend a full wash, called ghusl, when you become Muslim. It is a good thing to ask a scholar about when you take your shahada.

Do I have to tell my family straight away?

No. There is no fixed rule. See the guide on telling your family under When it gets hard.

What if I am not sure yet?

That is fine. Take your time, ask your questions first, and talk to someone you trust. We are happy to talk.

Core belief

Being checked with our scholars

Where it all starts. Who Allah is, what we believe, and why everything else rests on it.

Purpose and background

We were created to know Allah and worship Him alone: “I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me” (Quran 51:56). You cannot worship someone you do not know. That is why belief comes before every rule, and why the Prophet ﷺ taught belief first in Makkah.

The core answer

  • Allah is One. “Say: He is Allah, One. Allah, the Eternal Refuge. He neither begets nor is born, nor is there to Him any equivalent” (Quran 112:1-4).
  • Nothing is like Him. “There is nothing like Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing” (Quran 42:11).
  • Tawheed means Allah alone is our Lord and Creator, Allah alone is worshipped, and His names and attributes belong to Him alone.
  • He is near and merciful. He knows us better than we know ourselves, and His mercy is greater than our mistakes.
  • Six things we believe: Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and the divine decree (Sahih Muslim, hadith of Jibril).

Common questions

Why are there 99 names of Allah?

The Prophet ﷺ said that Allah has ninety-nine names, and whoever memorises and understands them enters Paradise (Sahih al-Bukhari 2736, Sahih Muslim 2677). We learn His names to know Him, to love Him, and to call on Him by them: “To Allah belong the best names, so call on Him by them” (Quran 7:180).

Do I have to understand everything before I pray or practise?

No. Learn the foundation, begin to practise, and keep learning. Allah does not burden a soul beyond what it can bear (Quran 2:286). If a question is bothering you, bring it to Questions and doubts or ask a person.

Prayer

Being checked with our scholars

Why we pray, how it works, and what to do when it is hard. Take it one step at a time.

Purpose and background

Prayer is how we live out why we were created. “Establish prayer for My remembrance” (Quran 20:14). Five times a day we stop and return to Allah, so the whole day is wrapped in remembrance of Him. It is also the first thing we will be asked about on the Day of Judgement. The Prophet ﷺ said: “The first action for which a servant will be held accountable is his prayer. If it is complete, he has succeeded; if it is defective, he has failed” (Jami’ at-Tirmidhi 413, graded hasan). The Quran adds that “prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing” (Quran 29:45).

It was given to the Prophet ﷺ on the night of the Isra and Mi’raj, when he was taken up to the heavens. The prayers were first fifty, and through Allah’s mercy were reduced to five, with the reward of fifty (Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim). It is a gift given directly, not through an angel.

The core answer: five prayers, every day

  • Fajr: dawn, before sunrise, 2 rakat. Dhuhr: after the sun passes its peak, 4. Asr: mid to late afternoon, 4. Maghrib: just after sunset, 3. Isha: night, after twilight ends, 4. That is 17 in total. These are the fard (obligatory) rakat. Some prayers also have extra sunnah rakat, and the schools of fiqh differ on a few details. That is for later.
  • In the first two rakat of every prayer you recite Al-Fatiha and a short surah. In the third and fourth, Al-Fatiha only.
  • Fajr, Maghrib and Isha are recited aloud in the first two rakat. Dhuhr and Asr are silent.
  • Before praying: make wudu, have clean clothes and a clean place, be covered, face the Kaaba, and wait until the time has begun.
  • Extra prayers (the sunnah prayers, nawafil and tahajjud) are a gift for later. For now, the five obligatory prayers are the anchor.

Walk through the prayer

What is said, what it means, where it comes from, and what it teaches. Read it once, then keep it nearby the first few times you pray.

The Quran text comes from Quran.com. The wording of the other parts was compared with Sunnah.com where we could. All of it is still to be reviewed by our scholars.

1. Niyyah: intention

Silent, in the heart. No spoken formula is required.

Source. The Prophet ﷺ said, “Actions are judged by intentions” (Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim). It is simply knowing, in your mind, which prayer you are standing for.

Meaning. This is the line between movement and worship. Without intention, standing, bowing and prostrating are just exercise. The intention is what makes it salah.

2. Takbir: opening the prayer

اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ

Allahu Akbar

“Allah is the Greatest.”

Source. Called Takbirat al-Ihram, the “prohibiting takbir”, because from here until the end of the prayer, talking, eating and looking around are not allowed. It is reported across Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim as how the Prophet ﷺ began every prayer.

Meaning. You declare, before anything else, that whatever you bring into this prayer, your day, your worries, your to-do list, is smaller than the One you stand before.

3. Qiyam: standing, and the opening du’a

سُبْحَانَكَ اللَّهُمَّ وَبِحَمْدِكَ، وَتَبَارَكَ اسْمُكَ، وَتَعَالَى جَدُّكَ، وَلَا إِلَهَ غَيْرُكَ

Subhanaka Allahumma wa bihamdika, wa tabarakasmuka, wa ta’ala jadduka, wa la ilaha ghairuk

“Glory to You, O Allah, and praise be to You. Blessed is Your name, exalted is Your majesty, and none is worthy of worship but You.”

Source. Reported from Abu Sa’id al-Khudri in Sunan Abi Dawud (775) and Jami’ at-Tirmidhi. The Abu Dawud report is about the Prophet’s ﷺ night prayer, and scholars also teach this opening for the obligatory prayers. Our scholars will confirm the wording.

Meaning. Before you recite a verse, you acknowledge who you are speaking to. Praise first, then request.

4. Recitation: Al-Fatiha, then a surah

بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ
ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ
ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ
مَـٰلِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ
إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ
ٱهْدِنَا ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ
صِرَٰطَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ غَيْرِ ٱلْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ

Bismillahir-Rahmanir-Rahim…

Surah Al-Fatiha (“The Opening”), the seven verses that open the Quran, then a second, shorter surah. Read and listen on Quran.com.

Source. The Prophet ﷺ said, “There is no prayer for the one who does not recite Al-Fatiha” (Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim). It is the one recitation that makes a rakah valid.

Meaning. Al-Fatiha is a conversation. According to a hadith qudsi, Allah responds to each line as you say it, before you ask for guidance in the last verse. It is the heart of each rakah.

5. Ruku: bowing

سُبْحَانَ رَبِّيَ الْعَظِيمِ

Subhana Rabbiyal ‘Adheem (3 times)

“Glory to my Lord, the Most Great.” Back flat, hands resting on the knees.

Source. Hudhayfah reported the Prophet ﷺ saying this in his bowing (Sahih Muslim 772). It echoes Quran 56:74, “So glorify the name of your Lord, the Most Great”. A report in Sunan Abi Dawud (869) says he told the Companions to use that verse in ruku, but Al-Albani graded that report weak, so we cite the verse for context only.

Meaning. The words of bowing echo a verse of the Quran. The link between the Quran and the prayer is close, not only symbolic.

6. Rising from ruku

سَمِعَ اللَّهُ لِمَنْ حَمِدَهُ، رَبَّنَا لَكَ الْحَمْدُ

Sami’ Allahu liman hamidah, Rabbana lakal hamd

“Allah hears the one who praises Him. Our Lord, all praise is for You.” Some reports add “wa”: Rabbana wa lakal hamd. Both are authentic.

Source. Reported by Abu Hurairah in Sahih al-Bukhari (789) and Sahih Muslim as the Prophet’s ﷺ own practice on rising from bowing.

Meaning. A brief upright pause, acknowledging that Allah has heard your praise, before the most humble posture of the prayer.

7. Sujood: prostration

سُبْحَانَ رَبِّيَ الْأَعْلَى

Subhana Rabbiyal A’la (3 times)

“Glory to my Lord, the Most High.” Forehead, nose, palms, knees and toes on the ground. Done twice in every rakah.

Source. Hudhayfah reported the wording (Sahih Muslim 772), which echoes Quran 87:1, “Exalt the name of your Lord, the Most High”. The Prophet ﷺ also said, “The closest a servant comes to his Lord is when he is prostrating, so increase your supplication in that state” (Sahih Muslim 482).

Meaning. This is the moment in your day when you are closest to Allah. That is why it is the recommended place to make personal dua, in whatever language you need.

8. Sitting between the two sujood

رَبِّ اغْفِرْ لِي، رَبِّ اغْفِرْ لِي

Rabbighfirli, Rabbighfirli

“My Lord, forgive me. My Lord, forgive me.” Said twice.

Source. Reported by Hudhayfah in Sunan Abi Dawud (874), graded sahih by Al-Albani. Ibn Abbas reports a longer version, “Allahummaghfir li warhamni…”, in Sunan Abi Dawud and Jami’ at-Tirmidhi.

Meaning. A brief seated breath between two acts of total submission, to simply ask forgiveness.

9. Tashahhud: at-Tahiyyat

التَّحِيَّاتُ لِلَّهِ وَالصَّلَوَاتُ وَالطَّيِّبَاتُ، السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكَ أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ وَرَحْمَةُ اللَّهِ وَبَرَكَاتُهُ، السَّلَامُ عَلَيْنَا وَعَلَى عِبَادِ اللَّهِ الصَّالِحِينَ، أَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَأَشْهَدُ أَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا عَبْدُهُ وَرَسُولُهُ

At-tahiyyatu lillahi was-salawatu wat-tayyibat, as-salamu ‘alayka ayyuhan-Nabiyyu wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh, as-salamu ‘alayna wa ‘ala ‘ibadillahis-salihin. Ashhadu an la ilaha illallah, wa ashhadu anna Muhammadan ‘abduhu wa rasuluh

“All greetings, blessings and pure words are for Allah. Peace be upon you, O Prophet, and the mercy of Allah and His blessings. Peace be upon us and upon the righteous servants of Allah. I bear witness there is no god but Allah, and I bear witness Muhammad is His servant and messenger.”

Source. Taught by the Prophet ﷺ to his Companions for every prayer. Reported from Ibn Mas’ud in Sahih al-Bukhari (831) and Sahih Muslim.

Meaning. Sat after the second rakah, and again at the end. It is your shahadah again, mid-prayer, renewed. The index finger is raised at “la ilaha illallah”, pointing to Allah’s oneness.

10. Durood: blessings on the Prophet

اللَّهُمَّ صَلِّ عَلَى مُحَمَّدٍ وَعَلَى آلِ مُحَمَّدٍ كَمَا صَلَّيْتَ عَلَى إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَعَلَى آلِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ إِنَّكَ حَمِيدٌ مَجِيدٌ، اللَّهُمَّ بَارِكْ عَلَى مُحَمَّدٍ وَعَلَى آلِ مُحَمَّدٍ كَمَا بَارَكْتَ عَلَى إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَعَلَى آلِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ إِنَّكَ حَمِيدٌ مَجِيدٌ

Allahumma salli ‘ala Muhammadin wa ‘ala ali Muhammad, kama sallayta ‘ala Ibrahima wa ‘ala ali Ibrahim, innaka Hamidun Majid. Allahumma barik ‘ala Muhammadin wa ‘ala ali Muhammad, kama barakta ‘ala Ibrahima wa ‘ala ali Ibrahim, innaka Hamidun Majid

“O Allah, send blessings upon Muhammad and the family of Muhammad, as You sent blessings upon Ibrahim and the family of Ibrahim. Indeed You are Praiseworthy and Glorious. O Allah, bless Muhammad and the family of Muhammad, as You blessed Ibrahim and the family of Ibrahim. Indeed You are Praiseworthy and Glorious.”

Source. Ka’b ibn Ujrah asked the Prophet ﷺ how to send salawat upon him. This is his answer (Sahih al-Bukhari 3370, Sahih Muslim).

Meaning. Recited only in the final sitting. It links you to the Prophet and, through him, to Ibrahim, a chain of prophets all conveying the same message.

11. Closing du’a

A short personal or Prophetic supplication before ending the prayer.

Meaning. The one place in the fixed structure where you can freely ask, in Arabic if you know it, or in your own words. Salah is structured, but not rigid at every point.

12. Salam: closing the prayer

السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكُمْ وَرَحْمَةُ اللَّهِ

Assalamu alaikum wa rahmatullah

“Peace and Allah’s mercy be upon you.” Turn the head to the right, then the left.

Source. Reported from Ibn Mas’ud in Sunan Abi Dawud (996), graded sahih by Al-Albani, and Jami’ at-Tirmidhi, as the standard closing of every prayer.

Meaning. Classical scholars explain it as greeting the two recording angels, and as your return to the world, carrying the peace of the prayer with you.

This covers the structure and the meaning of each part. It does not yet cover the finer fiqh: what is fard, wajib or sunnah, what invalidates a prayer, and where the schools of thought differ. Those differences are mostly about method, not meaning, and we will add them gradually, so it never feels like too much at once.

Why 2, 4, 4, 3 and 4 rakat?

The texts do not give a reason for the numbers, and we do not invent one. We pray them because Allah and His Messenger taught them: “It is not for a believing man or a believing woman, when Allah and His Messenger have decided a matter, that they should have any option in their decision” (Quran 33:36). Some parts of worship come with a wisdom we are told, and some we follow because they were commanded: “We hear and we obey” (Quran 2:285).

Why do we make wudu?

Because we are about to stand before Allah. Allah “loves those who purify themselves” (Quran 2:222), and the Prophet ﷺ said that cleanliness is half of faith (Sahih Muslim). Wudu also washes away sins as the water falls (Sahih Muslim). It is cleanliness of the body that prepares the heart.

Common struggles

I missed a prayer. What do I do?

The Prophet ﷺ said: whoever forgets a prayer or sleeps through it, its atonement is to pray it when he remembers (Sahih al-Bukhari 597, Sahih Muslim 684). Pray it as soon as you can, in order, and then carry on. Do not give up the rest of the day because one was missed.

Prayers from before you took your shahada are not made up. The scholars say Islam wipes away what came before. Ask a scholar about anything specific.

I have not learned the words yet

Keep praying with what you know and keep learning. A man once told the Prophet ﷺ that he could not learn the Quran, and asked for something to say instead. He taught him: Subhan Allah, walhamdulillah, la ilaha illa Allah, wallahu akbar, wa la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah (Sunan Abi Dawud). Learn Al-Fatiha and a short surah first, then add more. Your anchor or a brother at the masjid can sit with you.

How many rakat is each prayer, and which are loud?

Fajr 2 (loud), Dhuhr 4 (silent), Asr 4 (silent), Maghrib 3 (first two loud), Isha 4 (first two loud). See the core answer above.

I forgot how many rakat I prayed

It happens to everyone. There is a prostration of forgetfulness (sujood as-sahw) for this. Ask a teacher to show you in person, once, so it stays with you.

I am tired, or I keep sleeping through Fajr

This is the most common struggle, and it is a habit to build, not a verdict on your faith. The Prophet ﷺ said the devil ties three knots on the back of your head while you sleep; remembering Allah, making wudu and praying each undo one (Sahih al-Bukhari 1142, Sahih Muslim 776). See Unlearning and growing for a plan.

Prayer feels empty or like a chore

That is normal and it is not the end. Slow down, understand what you are saying, and ask Allah for help with it. See “Enjoy Your Prayer” below. The Prophet ﷺ said the coolness of his eyes was placed in prayer (Sunan an-Nasa’i, Musnad Ahmad).

Can I pray at work or in public?

Yes. The Prophet ﷺ said that the earth has been made a place of prayer for him (Sahih al-Bukhari 335, Sahih Muslim 521). Any clean place will do. Ask for a quiet room or corner. Most employers say yes when asked politely.

Zakat and giving

Being checked with our scholars

Why Islam makes giving a pillar, and how it works.

Purpose and background

Everything we own is a trust from Allah: “Spend from that in which He has made you successors” (Quran 57:7). Zakat purifies the wealth and the giver (Quran 9:103). It also keeps wealth moving through society, “so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich” (Quran 59:7). Picture a community in which every eligible person gave it. Need would be met, and no one would have to ask.

The Prophet ﷺ said that charity does not decrease wealth (Sahih Muslim 2588). It is one of the five pillars (Sahih al-Bukhari 8).

The core answer

  • Who gives: a Muslim whose savings (cash, gold, silver, investments, trade stock) stay above a set threshold, the nisab, for a full lunar year.
  • How much: 2.5% of that wealth. It is not on your income or your home. It is on what you have saved.
  • When: once a year. Many people choose Ramadan.
  • Who receives it: eight groups named in Quran 9:60, including the poor and the needy.
  • If you are new: you only give it once your savings pass the nisab and a year has passed. Nothing is owed backwards from before you were Muslim.
What is sadaqah, and how is it different?

Zakat is the fixed, obligatory share. Sadaqah is voluntary, any amount, any time. “Every good deed is charity” (Sahih Muslim 1005), including a kind word and a smile. You can give sadaqah with no savings at all.

How do I work out my zakat?

The nisab is set by the value of gold (about 85g), and some scholars use silver. The figure changes with prices, so use a reliable zakat calculator or ask a scholar. We will add a trusted calculator here once it is reviewed.

What is the example of the Prophet?

He ﷺ was the most generous of people, and most generous in Ramadan (Sahih al-Bukhari 6). He taught that giving makes wealth blessed, and he lived it.

Fasting and Ramadan

Being checked with our scholars

Why we fast, what Ramadan is, and how to go into your first one prepared.

Purpose and background

“O you who believe, fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, that you may attain taqwa” (Quran 2:183). Taqwa is awareness of Allah. Hunger teaches us self-restraint and gratitude, and lets us feel what people who go without feel every day.

Ramadan is the month the Quran was revealed (Quran 2:185). The Prophet ﷺ said that when it comes, the gates of Paradise are opened, the gates of the Fire are closed and the devils are chained (Sahih al-Bukhari 1899, Sahih Muslim 1079). Whoever fasts it with faith and hope for reward has their past sins forgiven (Sahih al-Bukhari 38, Sahih Muslim 760). Fasting is for Allah alone, and He gives its reward Himself (Sahih al-Bukhari 1904, Sahih Muslim 1151).

The core answer

  • What: from dawn (Fajr) until sunset (Maghrib), no food, no drink and no marital relations. Keep your tongue and your temper in check as well.
  • Suhoor: eat before dawn. “In suhoor there is blessing” (Sahih al-Bukhari 1923, Sahih Muslim 1095).
  • Iftar: break your fast at sunset. The Prophet ﷺ broke it with dates and water (Sunan Abi Dawud).
  • Who is excused: the sick, travellers, women with their cycle, and the very old. Make up the days later where you can (Quran 2:184-185).
  • The nights: taraweeh prayers, extra worship, and Laylat al-Qadr, better than a thousand months (Quran 97:3), in the last ten nights.
  • When: Ramadan 2027 is expected in early February. The start is confirmed by the moon sighting.
How do I get through my first Ramadan?

Take it a day at a time. Eat a proper suhoor, drink plenty between iftar and Fajr, and pace yourself. Tell your workplace early. Come to the masjid for iftar so you are not breaking your fast alone. If you slip or miss a day, you make it up and carry on. Ask us for a buddy: Talk to a person.

What do I do if I cannot fast?

If you are ill or travelling, the Quran gives you a way: “then an equal number of other days” (Quran 2:184). If it is a long-term condition, ask a scholar about fidyah. Do not force yourself to harm your health.

What is special about this month?

The Quran, the nights, giving, and community. It is also a good moment to leave a habit, because Ramadan carries you. See Unlearning and growing.

Unlearning and growing

Being checked with our scholars

Becoming Muslim means learning new things, and letting go of old ones. We do not pretend it is easy, and we do not pretend nothing needs to change.

Purpose and background

Islam does not say “you are Muslim now, so everything is fine as it is.” Some things you learn. Some things you unlearn. Allah gives us the means to do it: “He has succeeded who purifies it [the soul], and he has failed who instils it with corruption” (Quran 91:9-10). Allah also does not ask the impossible: “Fear Allah as much as you are able” (Quran 64:16). The Prophet ﷺ said the religion is easy, and no one makes it harder on himself without it overcoming him, so be steady and moderate (Sahih al-Bukhari 39).

Gradual is how you walk. It is not where you are headed. Iman first, then habits follow. Your old friends are not cut off, and you are not asked to become someone else. You are asked to move towards Allah, one step at a time.

How we help: three steps, use as many as you need

  • 1. A reminder. Sometimes motivation is all you need. Read the short reminder below and get back up.
  • 2. A plan. If you need more than a reminder, use the practical steps and the free resources.
  • 3. A person. If it still has a hold, do not carry it alone. Talk to someone.

Laziness, and missing prayers

Why it matters

Prayer is the line between the Muslim and everything else in the day. Laziness is a human habit, not a sin of character, and it is beaten by routine, not by feeling like it.

A reminder, for right now

The Prophet ﷺ said the devil ties three knots on your head as you sleep. Remember Allah and one knot comes undone. Make wudu and another. Pray and the last one, and you rise bright and light (Sahih al-Bukhari 1142, Sahih Muslim 776).

A plan

Set an alarm and put it across the room. Sleep earlier. Make wudu the moment you wake. Pray Fajr first, think later. Pray with a brother or at the masjid when you can. The five prayers are obligatory, so the aim is all five. If you are rebuilding, secure the one you miss most with an alarm and a person, and keep going until all five are in place. Drinking, and pub culture

Why it matters

Alcohol is forbidden because it clouds the mind, breeds enmity, and keeps a person from remembering Allah and from prayer (Quran 5:90-91). This one is not optional. It is also one many people find hard, because it is tied to friends, work and habit.

A reminder, for right now

The path is tawbah: stop, feel regret, and resolve not to return. “Allah is the Accepting of repentance, the Merciful” (Quran 9:104). Each day is a new start.

You do not have to cut off your friends. Tell them plainly that you are Muslim now and no longer drink. Most people respect it. Suggest meeting somewhere else: food, a walk, the gym. Do not go where the temptation is greatest while you are still finding your feet. Fill the evening with something else.

Pornography, and desire

Why it matters

Allah commands both believing men and believing women to lower their gaze and guard their chastity (Quran 24:30-31). This is for protection, not to shame anyone. Pornography is a habit that grows in secret, and secrecy is what keeps people stuck.

A reminder, for right now

“Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins” (Quran 39:53). Do not let shame become the reason you stay. Return, again and again if you must.

When the urge comes, get up, make wudu and pray two rakat, or go outside. Fill the time with something else. What we eat: halal food

Hajj

Being checked with our scholars

The pilgrimage to Makkah. It comes much later for most people, but it helps to know what it is.

Purpose and background

“Pilgrimage to the House is a duty owed to Allah by all who are able” (Quran 3:97). It was first called by Ibrahim and Isma’il, who built the Kaaba (Quran 22:27). It brings millions of Muslims of every race, language and background together in the same clothing, doing the same acts. It is a rehearsal for the Day we all stand before Allah.

The core answer

  • Obligatory once in a lifetime for those who are able, in health and in means. If you cannot afford it, it is not required of you.
  • When: the days of Dhul Hijjah, the last month of the Islamic year. Umrah, the shorter visit, can be done at other times.
  • The Day of Arafah (9 Dhul Hijjah) is the heart of Hajj. The Prophet ﷺ said “Hajj is Arafah” (Sunan at-Tirmidhi, Sunan Abi Dawud). There is no day on which Allah frees more people from the Fire (Sahih Muslim 1348).
  • If you are not on Hajj: fasting the Day of Arafah expiates the sins of the year before and the year after (Sahih Muslim 1162).

Special days

Being checked with our scholars

The two Eids and the best days of the year. What each one is, and what we do.

Purpose and background

Islam gives the year a rhythm: days of joy, days of remembrance, days of worship. Learning why each one exists keeps us from copying culture and shows us the Sunnah.

The two Eids

There are two: Eid al-Fitr, at the end of Ramadan, and Eid al-Adha, on 10 Dhul Hijjah. Before the Eid prayer: ghusl, your best clothes, and the takbir. Eid al-Fitr also has zakat al-fitr, a small charity given before the prayer so that everyone can celebrate (Sahih al-Bukhari 1503, Sahih Muslim 984). Eid al-Adha remembers Ibrahim’s obedience, when Allah ransomed his son with a sacrifice (Quran 37:102-107), and many Muslims give a qurbani.

Eid is not a day to be alone. If you have nowhere to go, tell us. Our aim is that nobody eats alone.

The first ten days of Dhul Hijjah

The Prophet ﷺ said that there are no days in which good deeds are more beloved to Allah than these ten days (Sahih al-Bukhari 969). Fast, pray, give and remember Allah more.

For later: Muharram and Ashura

This is for later. You do not need it to begin.

Muharram is the first month of the Islamic year. The Prophet ﷺ said that the best fasting after Ramadan is Allah’s month, Muharram (Sahih Muslim 1163). He fasted the 10th, Ashura, the day Allah saved Musa and his people (Sahih al-Bukhari 2004, Sahih Muslim 1130), and encouraged fasting a day with it.

What is the Sunnah and what is culture?

Many Muslim families celebrate in ways that come from their culture, not from the Prophet ﷺ. You do not have to copy any of it. Learn what the Prophet did, enjoy the day, and treat others’ customs with respect.

Marriage

Being checked with our scholars

Why Islam encourages marriage, what it asks of both people, and how to approach it with patience.

Purpose and background

“Among His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves, that you may find tranquillity in them, and He placed between you love and mercy” (Quran 30:21). Marriage protects chastity and lineage, and it gives a home in which the next generation grows up in faith. The Prophet ﷺ told those who can afford it to marry, for it lowers the gaze and protects chastity (Sahih al-Bukhari 5066, Sahih Muslim 1400).

The core answer

  • The marriage contract (nikah): the consent of both, a guardian (wali) for the bride, a mahr (gift) from the groom, witnesses, and an announcement.
  • Rights and responsibilities: each has rights over the other, and they are to be treated with kindness. “The best of you are the best to their wives” (Jami’ at-Tirmidhi, Sunan Ibn Majah). Quran 2:228 and 4:19 speak of fair treatment.
  • In the UK: a nikah on its own may not be recognised in law. Many couples also register a civil marriage. Check the current rules before you marry.
  • Outside marriage: Islam does not permit intimate relationships outside marriage (Quran 17:32). If you are already in a relationship, talk to a scholar privately and without fear.
Why this protects chastity and lineage

Marriage makes the bond clear, the rights known and the children’s lineage protected. Islam asks us to guard our chastity and lower our gaze, and it gives marriage as the lawful and honourable way (Quran 24:30-33).

I want to marry, or I am marrying because of someone

Take your time. “There is no compulsion in religion” (Quran 2:256), and nobody should marry to be accepted. Learn your faith, and know your own mind. Let a scholar or an older brother or sister help you.

Reverts and marriage: being turned down

This is a real pain point, and we do not hide it. Reverts can be turned down by families for cultural reasons that are not from Islam. The Prophet ﷺ said: if someone comes to you whose religion and character you are pleased with, marry him (Jami’ at-Tirmidhi). “The most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous” (Quran 49:13). See When it gets hard.

Family difficulties

Many families struggle with a conversion. Allah says: even if they strive to make you ascribe partners to Him, do not obey them in that, but “keep them company in this world with kindness” (Quran 31:15). Asma’ asked the Prophet ﷺ about her mother, who was not Muslim, and he told her to keep the ties (Sahih al-Bukhari 2620). You may want to wait until you are ready before you tell your family, and that is your decision. Ask us for support.

The words of Allah. You can start reading in English today and learn Arabic step by step.

Purpose and background

The Quran is the speech of Allah, sent down as guidance for all people (Quran 2:185). It is the answer to why we exist and how to live. The Prophet ﷺ said: the best of you are those who learn the Quran and teach it (Sahih al-Bukhari 5027). It is also a mercy and a healing for the hearts.

The core answer

  • Start small. Five minutes a day is enough. Read the meaning in English first, then listen to the Arabic.
  • Begin with Al-Fatiha, Al-Ikhlas, then the short surahs at the end. They are the ones you will use in prayer.
  • If Arabic is hard, you are rewarded for struggling. The one who recites with difficulty has two rewards (Sahih al-Bukhari 4937, Sahih Muslim 798).
  • Read it with understanding. Use a tafsir (explanation) a few verses at a time.

Your path with the Quran

Many people who come to Islam want to read the Quran in Arabic. You can. Begin at the first step and go at your own pace. Listening and reading the English can happen alongside.

  1. Learn the letters and their soundsThe Arabic alphabet, said out loud. A class or a teacher makes this much easier. Our own guide is coming soon.
  2. Learn to join them and read slowlyBeginner reading methods such as the Qaidah Nooraniyyah are taught in many masajid, to adults as well as children.
  3. Learn the rules of recitation, tajweed, graduallyThis is best learnt with a teacher who can hear you. Do not wait until it is perfect before you read.
  4. Read the meaningUse a translation alongside the Arabic, a few verses at a time (see the translations below).
  5. Go into tafsirThe explanation of the Quran. Start small, and see the tafsir note below.

The Prophet

Being checked with our scholars

Get to know the life of the Prophet ﷺ, the best example to follow and to love.

Purpose and background

Allah says: “You have in the Messenger of Allah an excellent example” (Quran 33:21), and that he was of “a great moral character” (Quran 68:4). The Prophet ﷺ said none of us truly believes until he is more beloved to us than our parents, our children and all people (Sahih al-Bukhari 15, Sahih Muslim 44). You cannot love someone you do not know. That is why we learn his life.

The core answer

  • Born in Makkah around 570 CE. At forty, the angel Jibril brought the first revelation (Quran 96:1-5, Sahih al-Bukhari 3).
  • Thirteen years of calling in Makkah, with patience under persecution. Then the Hijrah to Madinah in 622 CE, where the first community was built.
  • He passed away in 632 CE, having delivered the message. The way of life he taught is still alive.
  • Start with his character, because that is what drew people to him.

Moments in his life

The Night Journey (Isra and Mi’raj)

The Night Journey: Allah took the Prophet ﷺ from Makkah to Jerusalem and then up through the heavens (Quran 17:1, 53:1-18). It is when the five daily prayers were given. We learn from it that prayer is a gift from Allah. Scholars note that the date is not firmly established, so we do not fix an observance on it. Our scholars will confirm this.

Dua

Dua is simply speaking to Allah. You can do it in any language, in your own words, at any time. Here are a few short ones to begin with.

Purpose and background

Dua is worship. “Your Lord says: Call upon Me and I will respond to you” (Quran 40:60). “When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I respond to the call of the caller when he calls on Me” (Quran 2:186). The Prophet ﷺ said that dua is worship (Sunan Abi Dawud, Jami’ at-Tirmidhi). You pray five times a day, and dua is how you talk to Allah in between, about everything.

The core answer

  • Speak to Allah in any language, in your own words, at any time.
  • Begin by praising Allah and sending peace on the Prophet ﷺ, then ask, with certainty that He hears you.
  • Best times: the last third of the night (Sahih al-Bukhari 1145, Sahih Muslim 758), in sujood, when the servant is closest to his Lord (Sahih Muslim 482), and between the adhan and the iqamah.
  • There is a dua for nearly everything, taught by the Prophet ﷺ: morning and evening, eating, sleeping, travelling. Learn them one at a time.

Common questions

Short, honest answers. For anything about your own situation, please ask a scholar.

Do I have to change everything straight away?

No. Change comes gradually, and belief comes first. The Quran itself was revealed in stages. Nobody expects your whole life to flip overnight, and pressure usually works against you.

Do I need to learn Arabic first?

No. Many Muslims pray for years while still learning Arabic. Start with what you can, use a transliteration guide, and build up slowly.

Do I need to know how to pray before I take my shahadah?

No. The shahadah comes first, and prayer and the rest are learned along the way. Nobody is expected to arrive knowing everything.

Do I have to cut off my old friends?

Not automatically. Your friends are still your friends. Over time you will work out what to keep and what to change, with guidance, at your own pace.

When should I tell my family?

There is no fixed rule. It depends on your situation, including whether you can stand on your own feet. Talk it through with someone you trust before deciding. There is a guide on this under “When it gets hard”.

Is this only for people who have just reverted?

No. It is for anyone new to Islam, exploring it, or finding their way back, and for anyone who wants to support them.

When things get hard

Short guides on handling real situations with wisdom, one step at a time. These are first drafts, and we are having them reviewed with scholars. For anything about your own situation, please ask someone you trust.

Telling your family

There is no fixed rule for when or how. Anyone who gives you one is guessing. It depends on you and on your family.

Ask yourself honestly before you decide. Could I stand on my own feet if the reaction is hard, with housing, money and support? Do I know what I believe and why, well enough to answer simple questions calmly? Is there one person I can lean on whatever happens? Which family member is most likely to listen first?

When you are ready. Choose a calm moment, not an argument and not a big family day. Start with who you are, not with what they should change. Saying that you have found something that has made you calmer and kinder lands very differently from a list of new rules. Expect questions and some hurt. The first reaction is often not the last, so do not argue to win.

Over time, your character does most of the talking. If parents push you away from your belief, the Quran says not to obey them in that, but to keep them company in this world with kindness (Quran 31:15). Kindness and firmness can sit together.

If you cannot pray openly at home yet, ask a scholar about your situation, rather than guess. And you do not have to carry any of this by yourself. Talk to your buddy or the circle before and after.

Culture and religion, and telling them apart

One of the most confusing parts of joining the Muslim community is working out which things are Islam and which are the habits of the people around you. Many Muslims were born into a culture and mix the two without noticing.

Food, dress, wedding customs, how families treat each other, who marries whom, what is seen as shameful. Some of it is harmless tradition. Some of it conflicts with Islam. Much of it is neutral. You do not have to guess.

A useful question to ask. Where does this come from, the Quran and the Sunnah, or from where someone grew up? Ask a scholar, not just whoever is closest. And Islam does not ask you to erase your background. Whatever does not conflict with it can stay.

Do not judge Islam by people. People will disappoint you, and Allah does not. If someone treats you as lesser because of where you come from, that is not Islam. The Quran puts the measure in one place: “The most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you” (Quran 49:13).

Friends who don’t understand

Your friends are still your friends. Becoming Muslim does not mean cutting everyone off, and it should not happen overnight.

Some friends will be curious, some will be awkward, and some will drift. All of that is normal. Tell them simply, something like “I’ve started praying, and it matters to me.” No lecture needed.

Start with small, steady things, like your prayer times and what you eat and drink. Some settings will get harder, especially ones centred on alcohol. Work out step by step, with guidance, what you can stay in and what you cannot, and offer alternatives: a football match, a meal, a walk.

Expect that a few will drift, and that it will hurt. Build new friendships alongside the old ones so there is no gap: a class, a sport, the circle. And do not look down on friends who are where you used to be. When you are unsure about a particular situation, ask one trusted brother or sister.

Letting go of old habits, and waking for Fajr

Almost everyone who comes to Islam carries habits they will need to change, from a late night routine to drinking to the way they spend their weekends. Change comes in stages, and so does the Quran itself. Nobody expects it all to go on the first day, and shame is the thing most likely to make people quit.

Small and steady beats big and short. The Prophet ﷺ said the most beloved deeds to Allah are the most consistent, even if small (Sahih al-Bukhari 6464, Sahih Muslim 783). Praying on time, kept up, is worth more than a week of perfect intentions.

Fajr is hard for most people, and it is not only you. Go to sleep earlier than you think you need to. Put your alarm across the room. Ask one person to message you in the morning. Join a Fajr accountability group in our community. If you do oversleep, ask a scholar what to do instead of guessing, and keep going the next day.

Letting go of a habit, such as drinking. Change the place and the time first, because habits live in routines. Decide what you will do in that gap instead: a walk, the gym, a call, a class. Tell one trusted person so you are not fighting it alone. Expect to slip. When you do, repent and carry on. Allah says: “Do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins” (Quran 39:53).

Unlearning also means putting down shame. You are not starting from a bad place. You are moving towards a better one, one step at a time.

Spending Eid / Christmas alone

If you are spending Eid or Christmas alone, you are far from the only one. It is one of the most common and least talked about parts of coming to Islam. Eid is a day built around family and gathering. Christmas may be the one time your old family traditions feel loudest. Both can leave you feeling you have lost something and not yet found what replaces it. That feeling is normal. It is not a sign you made a mistake.

Plan early, not on the day. A day with a shape is far easier than an empty one.

For Eid. Go to the Eid prayer and arrive early. Say salaam to one person and introduce yourself, most people are glad to be asked. Message a mentor, buddy or the circle a week before and ask who you can spend the afternoon with. Eid meals are often open to anyone, and a quick message makes all the difference. Have something small planned either way: a meal, a walk, a call.

For Christmas. You may be invited to family gatherings. Scholars give different answers on particular things such as gifts, cards or attending a meal, so ask someone you trust instead of guessing. What nearly everyone agrees on is that you keep family ties and stay kind. Allah says He does not forbid you from being kind and fair to those who have not fought you over your religion (Quran 60:8). You can be warm, grateful and present without making the day a debate. If you will be on your own anyway, arrange to spend part of the day with other Muslims.

If it still hurts, say so to someone. Feeling lonely at these times is not weakness. We are building a plan so no one in our circle is left alone on these days.

Marriage, and being accepted

For many new Muslims, marriage is where being new feels heaviest. Some families see a revert as a risk, however sincere he or she is. And some reverts, wanting to belong, rush into a match that is not right. Both are real, and neither is a reason to lose heart.

Slow down on purpose. Marrying quickly out of loneliness is one of the most common regrets. Take time to know the person and their family, and ask people who know them well.

What Islam asks you to look for. The Prophet ﷺ said: “If someone whose religion and character you are pleased with comes to you, then marry him” (Jami’ at-Tirmidhi 1084). The measure is religion and character, for you as much as for them. It is not family name, background or where you were born.

If you are treated as a risk. That is people, not Islam. Allah puts the measure in one place: “The most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you” (Quran 49:13). Over time, steady practice, good character and people who will speak for you carry more weight than any argument.

Bring someone in. Do not carry this alone. Talk it through with a mentor, an imam or a trusted elder, and let them be part of the conversations with families. The details of a marriage, including the guardian (wali), witnesses and the mahr, are for a scholar to explain to your situation. This matters especially for sisters whose families are not Muslim, so please ask early.

After the shahada: why we built Revert Journeys

Essay. About 7 minutes.

A man takes his shahadah on a Friday evening. The room is warm. Brothers embrace him, someone records a video, someone else cries. For a moment, he is the most celebrated person in the building.

Then everyone goes home.

What happens on the Tuesday after is the real story, and almost nobody tells it. No camera is rolling when he is trying to work out how to pray without anyone watching him get it wrong. No one is clapping when he sits at his family’s dinner table, deciding whether tonight is the night he says something, or whether it can wait. The warmth of the room does not travel home with him.

That gap, between the celebration of a shahadah and the quiet work of living a new life, is why Revert Journeys exists.

Two layers, not one

Most support for new Muslims covers one layer: the practical one. How to make wudu. How to pray. What the five pillars are. It matters, but beneath it sits a harder layer, the emotional and relational one, where a person learns to navigate people, not just rituals.

How do you tell your mother? How do you sit at a table where everyone is drinking and you are not, without making it a scene? How do you build a new circle of friends when the old one does not disappear, and should not have to?

Belief has to be grounded first. Once someone is grounded in what he believes and why, everything after it, the difficult conversations, the friends who drift, the family who do not understand, becomes part of the test rather than a reason to abandon the path.

What we learned from someone who has done this before

Early on we sat with a guest who has thought hard about how spaces like this should be built. He told us to build in levels, not one room for everyone, because a revert three weeks in and a revert three years in are not having the same experience. He told us mentors grow from within: reverts who have sat in class for a long time, watching the questions go back and forth, become mentors almost without noticing.

He also said the shahadah is not a finished stamp. A person can say the words and still not know what they mean by heart. And he said something harder: as reverts, we have to take our own Islam into our own hands. Blaming other people or the environment is not always fair, so we push for knowledge and stay involved in the deen.

One practical gap he named has stayed with us as a commitment: Christmas and Eid. Reverts spending those days alone is common, and it should not be. Food, people, somewhere to be.

You are still on your own

We can build structure, mentors and a real community, and all of it matters. But underneath, a person is still on his own. That is not a failure of the system, it is simply true for everyone. It means decisions are made with wisdom, not outsourced. When to tell family is not a fixed rule. It depends on whether you can stand on your own two feet. Support should inform that wisdom, never replace it.

Everyone was a revert once

Every companion of the Prophet ﷺ was, at some point, a revert. The idea of the heritage Muslim, raised entirely inside the culture of Islam, is in a real sense the newer one. Being born Muslim does not automatically mean more grounding or knowledge. A revert of twenty years, studying and striving the whole time, can be far more grounded than someone who has never had to work for any of it.

Islam is built on effort, not inheritance. The Prophet ﷺ said the one who recites the Quran with ease is with the noble angels, and the one who struggles through it has two rewards (Sahih al-Bukhari 4937, Sahih Muslim 798).

Two journeys, two lenses

A revert’s journey tends to be a search. He is in pursuit, looking inward for purpose. A heritage Muslim’s test often runs the other way, less an inward search than an outward resistance, an environment pulling against what he already has. Neither is easier. Recognising which lens someone is looking through is what makes empathy real instead of performed.

And different communities carry different pain points. A Black revert, a South Asian revert, an Arab revert and a white British revert are not having the same experience.

Allah said, “The most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you” (Quran 49:13). The Prophet ﷺ repeated it in his final sermon: no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, or of the red over the dark, except by taqwa (Musnad Ahmad 23489).

People will disappoint you. If you build your understanding of this religion on anyone’s example, you will eventually be let down, because everyone involved is human. Allah does not disappoint. That is the anchor under everything we are building.

Not a tick box for one evening of celebration, but something sturdy enough to still be standing on the Tuesday after, and the Tuesday after that, for as long as it takes.

How we support

The way we treat people is part of the message. These six habits are for everyone who helps here.

We listen first

Before we say anything, we ask what you need and what is hard right now.

We keep it short

The Prophet ﷺ was given concise, comprehensive speech (Sahih Muslim 523). We answer the question that was asked.

We never pressure or argue

“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction” (Quran 16:125).

We meet you where you are

New, returning, scholarly or simple. The road is different for each person.

We show it, not just say it

Our character is part of what we are inviting people to.

We make dua

Guidance is Allah’s (Quran 28:56). Support is ours.
